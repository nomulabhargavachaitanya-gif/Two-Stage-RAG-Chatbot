British mathematician and logician Alan Turing laid foundational principles for theoretical computer science, cryptanalysis, and machine intelligence. In his 1936 paper "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem," Turing introduced an abstract theoretical computing machine consisting of an infinite paper tape scanned by a read-write head operating under finite state transitions. This model, now known as the universal Turing machine, established the mathematical definition of algorithmic computation and resolved David Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem in the negative by proving that the Halting Problem is undecidable.

During the Second World War, Turing worked at Bletchley Park within Hut 8, focusing on cryptanalysis of cipher messages from the German naval Enigma machine. Together with Gordon Welchman, Turing designed the electromechanical Bombe machine, which systematically eliminated invalid rotor combinations using known-plaintext segments known as cribs, dramatically reducing the manual search space for decryption keys and expediting allied intelligence extraction.

Following the war, Turing designed the Automatic Computing Engine (ACE) at the National Physical Laboratory in 1945, presenting one of the earliest designs for a stored-program digital computer that utilized acoustic mercury delay lines for high-speed dynamic memory storage. In 1950, Turing published his seminal philosophical paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in Mind, introducing the imitation game—later termed the Turing Test—as an operational benchmark for determining whether an artificial system exhibits human-equivalent intelligence. Turing spent his final years developing mathematical biology, authoring "The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis" in 1952, which demonstrated how reaction-diffusion systems between morphogens spontaneously produce organic patterns such as stripes and whorls.
