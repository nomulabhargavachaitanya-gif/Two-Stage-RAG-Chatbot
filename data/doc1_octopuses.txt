Octopuses are marine animals known for their intelligence, flexible bodies, and eight arms. They live in oceans around the world, usually sheltering in rocky crevices, coral reefs, or other protected spaces. Most species are solitary and spend much of their time searching for crabs, fish, and other small prey. An octopus has three hearts: two branchial hearts pump blood through each of the two gills, while a third systemic heart pumps blood through the rest of the body. Their blood uses copper-rich hemocyanin rather than iron-based hemoglobin to transport oxygen, giving octopus blood a distinctive blue color.

An octopus can rapidly alter the color, pattern, and texture of its skin to blend into its surroundings or communicate with conspecifics. This camouflage ability is controlled by specialized dermal cells called chromatophores, which are elastic pigment sacs surrounded by radial muscle fibers under direct neural control. Beneath the chromatophores lie iridophores, which produce structural iridescent greens and blues by reflecting light, and leucophores, which scatter white light across a broad spectrum. Camouflage helps the animal avoid predators such as moray eels and approach prey without detection.

Octopuses are also exceptionally skilled problem solvers with a decentralized nervous system. Two-thirds of an octopus's neurons are located in the nerve cords of its arms rather than its central brain, allowing each arm to explore, taste, and manipulate objects semi-autonomously. Because they lack both an internal skeleton and an external shell, an octopus can squeeze its soft body through remarkably narrow crevices, provided the opening is large enough to accommodate its hard chitinous beak, which is the only rigid component of its body.
