Deep-sea hydrothermal vents are fissures on oceanic tectonic plates where volcanically heated mineral fluids discharge into the deep ocean abyss. They were first discovered in 1977 along the Galapagos Rift near the East Pacific Rise by oceanographers diving aboard the research submersible Alvin. The discovery fundamentally revised biological paradigms by proving that complex macroscopic animal communities can flourish in perpetual darkness without direct reliance on sunlight or solar photosynthesis.

Hydrothermal vents typically form along divergent plate boundaries where cold seawater seeps deep into basaltic oceanic crust, encounters magma reservoirs, and superheats up to 400 degrees Celsius under hydrostatic pressure. As this superheated fluid ascends and mixes with freezing bottom seawater, dissolved metals precipitate out as metal sulfides, forming towering mineral chimneys. Black smokers emit dark plumes rich in iron monosulfide precipitate, whereas white smokers discharge cooler, acidic fluids dominated by barium, calcium, and silica complexes.

Vent ecosystems rely entirely on chemosynthesis driven by chemolithoautotrophic bacteria and archaea. These microbes oxidize dissolved hydrogen sulfide or methane to fix inorganic carbon into organic matter. Among the most iconic endemic organisms is the giant tube worm Riftia pachyptila, which can attain lengths of over two meters. Adult tube worms possess neither a mouth, gut, nor digestive tract; instead, they harbor billions of sulfur-oxidizing endosymbionts in a vascularized internal organ termed the trophosome. The worms' bright red plumes contain specialized high-molecular-weight hemoglobin molecules that bind both hydrogen sulfide and molecular oxygen simultaneously, transporting them safely through the bloodstream without metabolic poisoning. Vent environments also host Pompeii worms (Alvinella pompejana), which inhabit chimney walls enduring temperatures up to 80 degrees Celsius.
